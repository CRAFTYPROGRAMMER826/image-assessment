First-time visitor guide

How to read your image-quality result

The score, issue percentages, severity, and status answer different questions. This guide explains each one and shows exactly how the current scoring system combines them.

The four numbers you are seeing

DisplayWhat it meansWhat it does not mean
Issue confidenceHow strongly the learned model believes an issue is present.It is not the percentage of pixels affected.
SeverityAn application band—low, medium, or high—assigned after detection.It is not a separate model or physical measurement.
Quality scoreEstimated quality retained after weighted deductions from 100.It is not the model's confidence that the image is “good.”
StatusA label derived from the final score.It is not an independent prediction.

Why issue percentages can exceed 100% together

Blur, noise, and underexposure are independent, coexisting conditions. A result can therefore be 90% confident in blur and 80% confident in noise at the same time. The values are not slices of a pie and must not be added as pixel coverage.

Think of weather: it can be 90% likely to rain, 80% likely to be windy, and 70% likely to be cold. Those events can all occur together.

The confidence bars on the analysis page are deliberately separate. A stacked 100% confidence bar would falsely imply that the issues are mutually exclusive.

How the overall score is calculated

The learned models output issue probabilities. A transparent application layer converts them into point deductions:

score = clamp(100 − Σ(issue probability × maximum penalty), 0, 100)
IssueMaximum deduction
Blur20 points
Underexposure20 points
Overexposure20 points
Noise15 points
Severe degradation25 points
Potential visual anomaly20 points
Important: probabilities below their detection threshold still make a smaller score deduction. This keeps the score gradual instead of jumping abruptly at a threshold.

Worked example: why 99% blur may still score around 77

Issue confidence controls only a fraction of that issue's maximum penalty. A 99% blur probability removes about 19.8 points—not 99 points.

Blur: 99% × 20−19.80 Overexposure: 10% × 20−2.00 Noise: 5% × 15−0.75 Severe degradation: 2% × 25−0.50 Total deduction−23.05 Quality retained76.95 ≈ 77

The image is labelled DEGRADED because 77 falls inside the degraded score band, even though blur itself has 99% confidence.

For detected issues, confidence below 70% is shown as low severity, 70–84% as medium, and 85%+ as high. Detection thresholds are target-specific and were selected using validation data; therefore “not detected” does not necessarily mean 0% confidence.

Does the model know which pixels are wrong?

No. The current model classifies the complete image using 19 global statistics. It can say that the overall feature pattern resembles blur, noise, or exposure damage, but it cannot claim that a specific percentage of pixels is defective or identify a defective region.

Pixel- or region-level statements would require a separately trained patch-localization or segmentation system. Exposure clipping ratios are genuine pixel proportions, but issue confidence values are not.

A practical reading order

  1. Start with the overall score and status to understand general usability.
  2. Look for issue cards marked detected.
  3. Use confidence to judge model certainty and severity to understand the display band.
  4. Read “Why this result?” and the plain-English summary for supporting measurements.
  5. Treat borderline results as review prompts, especially for intentionally dark, bright, grainy, or shallow-depth-of-field photography.

One final rule of thumb

Confidence answers “how sure?”, score answers “how usable overall?”, and clipping ratios answer “how many pixels?”. They are related, but they are not interchangeable.
